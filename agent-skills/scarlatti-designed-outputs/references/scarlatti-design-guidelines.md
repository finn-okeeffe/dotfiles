## 2. Brand character

Scarlatti is a research, evaluation, data, facilitation and practical-action brand. The visual result should feel:

- **clear and capable** — structured information, plain language and strong hierarchy;
- **warm and human** — real people, real work and approachable detail, rather than corporate gloss;
- **analytical but usable** — evidence is made decision-ready, not merely displayed;
- **quietly confident** — ample whitespace, restrained colour and no decorative clutter;
- **active and outcome-focused** — show a path from question or evidence to a useful next step.

The current Scarlatti values provide a useful check on this expression: **Ambition** (embrace ambiguity to create opportunities), **Fairy Dust** (bring creativity and diversity of thought), **Excellence** (produce high-quality work through continuous iteration) and **Heart** (care about the work and be authentic).

The public site’s expression is particularly useful for digital work: short, plain-English headlines; a concise explanation; a clear call to action; warm real-world imagery; and modular service, proof and capability sections. It avoids hype, stock-photo theatre and dense marketing language.

| Secondary | Deep blue | `#084C61` | Infographic/chart series and dark contrast field |
| Secondary | Blue teal | `#177E89` | Infographic/chart series |

| Secondary | Light peach | `#F8DDC4` | Light fill and warm supporting panel |
| Secondary | Mustard | `#E7B953` | Infographic/chart series; use sparingly |
| Secondary | Taupe grey | `#726C60` | Table header and muted neutral |
| Special positive | Green | `#79A83D` | Good/positive status or Green portfolio only |
| Special negative | Red-orange | `#E4573F` | Bad/negative status only |

### Rules for colour

1. **Orange is the hero, not the background default.** It should be visibly present in a Scarlatti artefact, but use it to direct attention: bullets, a subtitle, a key word, an icon, a rule or a focused data series.
2. Let the primary palette dominate. Use secondary colours principally to distinguish data, categories and information layers.
3. Start charts with orange, teal and charcoal; then add the secondary colours in a logical, stable order. For five or more distinguishable categories, expand with deep blue, blue teal, sand, mustard and/or light tones as needed.
4. Use tints (approximately 25%, 50%, 75% or 90%) of an approved colour to create hierarchy before introducing an unrelated colour.
5. Limit most layouts to two or three prominent shape/fill colours. A chart can need more, but its meaning must remain clear.
6. Use `#726C60` for the first/header row of standard tables. Keep table bodies white or unfilled unless a restrained fill adds meaning.

### Contrast and accessibility

- Treat `#E4813F` and `#1B998B` as accents on white, **not** as normal-size text. Their contrast on white is below the 4.5:1 normal-text target in the brand accessibility check.
- For normal text, favour black/charcoal on white or cream, or white on a sufficiently dark field (for example deep blue or charcoal). Use black text on orange or light teal where appropriate.
- Do not use colour alone to communicate good/bad, category, priority or selection. Add labels, patterns, icons or direct data labels.
- Check the final contrast, especially after adding transparency, photos or partner colours. Aim for WCAG AA: 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text.

## 5. Typography

### Core type choices

- **Calibri Light** is the working font for body copy, tables, charts and most functional document text. Use Calibri Light rather than plain Calibri where it is available.
- **Roboto Bold** is used for the tagline and is the display face for prominent section/cover headings and web heading contexts.
- Do not introduce a third display font merely for decoration. If a platform cannot provide a font, use its closest approved template fallback and flag the substitution if it affects a deliverable.

### Word reports and proposals

Start from the current Word template and use its named styles. Do not recreate styles by direct formatting.

| Element | Default treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Body | Calibri Light, 11 pt, black, justified; 8 pt space after |
| Heading 1 | Calibri Light, bold, black, 18 pt; orange 0.5 pt bottom rule |
| Heading 2 | Calibri Light, bold, orange, 16 pt |
| Heading 3 | Calibri Light, bold, black, 14 pt |
| Heading 4 | Calibri Light, bold, black, 12 pt |
| Heading 5 | Calibri Light, 11 pt, italic |
| Bullets/numbers | Calibri Light, 11 pt black body with orange bullet/number |
| Caption | Calibri Light, 11 pt, centred, italic |
| Footnote | Calibri Light, 9 pt |
| Table body | Calibri Light, 11 pt, black |
| Cover/section display | Use the existing current-template styles (including Roboto display styles) rather than manual substitutions |

Use sentence case for headings unless the user supplies an approved proper name or title. Keep headings informative, specific and short enough to scan. Do not rely on colour as the only hierarchy signal.

### PowerPoint

Choose the right mode before designing:

- **Printable/report PowerPoint:** body copy is Calibri Light, generally 11 pt or 14 pt consistently; slide titles are bold black 18 pt; use a bold orange 11 pt section label above the title.
- **Presentation-only PowerPoint:** use the current 16:9 presentation template. Its guidance supports Roboto Bold headings (typically 32–36 pt) and Calibri Light supporting text (typically 16–20 pt, never below 14 pt).

In both modes, use sentence case; align body text left and commonly vertically centre it; keep copy brief; and use the existing slide layouts rather than adding ad-hoc body text boxes.

## 6. Layout and composition

### Universal layout rules

- Begin with the appropriate supplied template. Preserve the slide master, document styles, margins, grids, headers and footers.
- Lead with one clear message per page/slide. Use a descriptive title, then make the evidence or next step easy to find.
- Prefer a strong grid, generous margins and whitespace to boxed-in layouts. In most slides, use roughly two-thirds of the available area rather than filling every corner.
- Create hierarchy through position, scale, weight and whitespace before adding colour, lines or containers.
- Align objects precisely. Group related objects before moving or scaling them. Make repeated elements identical in size, spacing and alignment.
- Use the orange detail/rule/bullet to provide a recognisable Scarlatti thread, without turning the page orange.
- Avoid gradients, decorative shadows, stock UI ornaments, excessive rounded cards, crowded collages and unexplained visual metaphors.

### Reports and documents

- Use section breaks/pages deliberately: a clear display heading, restrained cover treatment and a readable contents page.
- Use orange rules/bottom borders from the template to separate levels rather than inventing extra dividers.
- Keep paragraphs short and lists purposeful. Long, dense prose should become subheadings, a table, a process, an evidence callout or an appendix where appropriate.
- Keep figure/table titles and notes with their object. Captions are centred and italic; provide a source where required.

### Slides

- A slide should state an insight, decision or question — not simply a topic. Prefer “Survey completion fell after the reminder changed” to “Survey results”.
- Keep a logical visual system across a deck. Section/border colours may change by section only if the convention is consistent and meaningful.
- Orange bullets and numbering are standard. Keep every bullet level aligned under its parent text.
- Use a rounded-rectangle crop for photos added to presentation slides, matching the template treatment. Retain enough empty space that the slide can breathe.

## 7. Data visualisation, tables and diagrams

### Charts

- Select the simplest chart that answers the question: bar/column for comparison, line for time, stacked bar/column for composition, and a pie only for a small number of clear parts of a whole.
- Use the supplied chart templates where available. If a chart is inserted from scratch, reformat it fully; Office defaults are not Scarlatti defaults.
- Use Calibri Light and black labels. In printable reports, chart labels are generally 11 pt; chart titles may be 11 or 14 pt, but stay consistent across the document.
- Remove unnecessary borders, heavy gridlines, 3D effects, gradients, legends that duplicate direct labels, and decimal precision that does not change the decision.
- Prefer direct labels and a short insight-led chart title. Show units, time period, denominator and source where needed.
- Use colour deliberately: highlight the focal series in orange; set context series in teal/charcoal/muted approved tones. Keep series colours stable across the document.
- Do not encode a positive/negative judgement with orange. Reserve green `#79A83D` and red-orange `#E4573F` for explicit good/bad status meanings.

### Tables

- Keep tables centred when they are the main element on a page/slide.
- Use a taupe-grey (`#726C60`) main heading row with bold black or white text as the template supports. Use only a small number of supporting coloured subheadings.
- Keep body cells white/no fill in most cases. Use Calibri Light black text; left-align prose and align numeric values consistently (usually right-aligned).
- Reduce columns, abbreviate labels sensibly, wrap text cleanly and move caveats to a note. A table that needs tiny text should be split, summarised or placed in an appendix.

### Icons, processes and infographics

- Use one icon family in a single artefact: either solid or outline, never a mixture. Keep stroke, colour and size consistent.
- Use primary or secondary palette colours and keep icons secondary to the message.
- Use simple arrows, chevrons, callouts and rounded rectangles from the template where a process needs them. Show sequence, ownership or decision points explicitly; decorative diagrams are not enough.
- Limit an infographic to the few relationships a reader needs to understand. Direct labels are usually better than a legend.

## 8. Imagery and digital expression

Use the website as the clearest reference for current digital tone:

- Prefer authentic, well-lit images of people thinking, discussing, facilitating, researching, using evidence or working with clients.
- Choose real settings, natural materials and an Aotearoa-relevant context where appropriate. The internal website library often uses warm wood, daylight, people in action and visible but not dominant research/data tools.
- Compose photos with usable negative space for copy, while keeping people and important objects clear. Use imagery that reflects the people affected by the work, not just generic office scenes.
- Use supplied images/logos before sourcing replacements. Confirm usage rights and accessibility alt text for new assets.
- Avoid posed handshakes, generic skyline/laptop stock photos, sci-fi AI imagery, sterile lab aesthetics, tokenistic cultural imagery, and image treatments that make evidence feel sensationalised.

For web or social concepts, use a modular sequence: clear proposition, short explanation, service/proof cards or sections, evidence of capability, then a direct action. Calls to action should be specific and plain (for example, “Explore our services” or “Get in touch”), not hype-led.

## 9. Writing that supports the design

Write in New Zealand English unless the project specifies otherwise. The tone is direct, thoughtful, evidence-led and useful.

- Start with the audience’s question, decision or desired outcome.
- Make claims specific and explain what the evidence enables someone to do.
- Prefer short declarative sentences, active verbs and familiar language. Define technical terms where an external reader needs them.
- Be accurate about uncertainty, methods and limitations. Do not inflate outcomes, invent client results, or claim causal impact without evidence.
- Avoid empty consulting language such as “unlock value”, “game-changing”, “world-class” or “seamless end-to-end solutions”.
- Use headings in sentence case; use serial commas only where they prevent ambiguity; use Māori macrons correctly when provided or readily verified.

## 10. Agent workflow and quality gate

Before producing a Scarlatti deliverable:

1. Identify the medium, audience, purpose, client co-branding and accessibility needs.
2. Start from the closest current template and keep its styles/master intact.
3. Use the active palette and supplied logo asset; do not copy a logo from an existing report or a screenshot.
4. Establish hierarchy first, then use orange as a controlled emphasis.
5. For data, decide the reader’s question and then use the simplest chart/table/diagram that answers it.
6. Run this final check:
   - Is the main message clear in five seconds?
   - Is the logo variant appropriate, correctly placed and surrounded by clear space?
   - Are fonts, size, case, colours, icon family and spacing consistent?
   - Is orange present but restrained, and do primary colours dominate?
   - Does all normal-size text meet contrast requirements or have an approved exception?
   - Are charts readable, truthful, labelled and free of default Office styling?
   - Are images authentic, relevant, licensed and supplied with meaningful alt text where needed?
   - Does the document feel spacious, practical and confidently understated?

If an existing client template conflicts with these rules, retain the client template and ask the user which brand should lead. Do not “Scarlatti-ise” a client-controlled report without permission.
